The Battle of Okinawa

Put together in a timeline.

Nathan Wong

12 June 2025

Okinawa, Japanese Empire

1945

"Ever since they turned the tide at Midway and got increasingly close to the Japanese home islands, the United States eyed Okinawa with interest. A set of strategic islands right at the doorstep of mainland Japan, Okinawa could be used to bomb major Japanese cities, unlike tedious bombing runs from distant bases like Guam or Saipan. The Japanese, seeing defeat was increasingly unavoidable, staged more extreme attacks on U.S. ships to the point where pilots were encouraged to fly their planes into enemy ships. It was not just warfare; it was ritual sacrifice, delivered from the sky. America now has two choices: launch a costly invasion that could kill hundreds of thousands—or find a way to end the war without setting foot on Japan’s soil."

The Battle of Okinawa (April 1 – June 22, 1945) was the largest and bloodiest amphibious assault of the Pacific War. Its goal was to seize Okinawa, the largest of Japan’s Ryukyu Islands, as a base for pounding the Japanese homeland and preparing for a planned invasion of Kyushu. 350 miles south of Japan’s main islands, Okinawa was the “crucial final stepping stone” toward Japan. By capturing Okinawa, the Allies aimed to cut Japan off from its Asian supply lines and set up airfields for long-range bombers. With Okinawa in hand, U.S. forces could unleash massive air strikes on Japan, blockade its ports, and stage the final invasion (codenamed Operation Olympic).

Planning

  • Early 1945 Allies plan massive amphibious invasion: Operation Iceberg.

    Allied planners (codename Operation Iceberg) prepared the largest Pacific amphibious invasion to date. Adm. Spruance’s Fifth Fleet and Lt. Gen. Buckner’s 10th Army trained for an 182,000‑man landing force. U.S. carriers and battleships launched round‑the‑clock strikes on Okinawa and nearby islands in February–March, destroying hundreds of Japanese planes. Japanese Commander Lt. Gen. Ushijima Mitsuru fortified southern Okinawa with ~100,000 troops in deep caves and ridge lines (the “Shuri Line”), readying an attrition defense. U.S. planners anticipated heavy losses and aimed to overwhelm defenders with massive bombardment and overwhelming force.

  • March 26, 1945 Kerama Islands under U.S. control.

    U.S. Marines land on the nearby Kerama and Keise Islets (15 miles west of Okinawa) to seize harbors and anchorages. Over 350 Japanese small boats (prepared for suicide attacks) are captured. American 155-mm artillery on Keise Islets is positioned to support the main assault.

  • Late March, 1945 Allied demolition teams and minesweepers clear obstacles from the Okinawan beaches.

    Carrier Task Force 58 (Adm. Mitscher) strikes Japanese airfields to suppress enemy air cover. By month’s end, the invasion fleet (1,600+ ships, 350,000 naval personnel) is assembled off Okinawa.

Battle of Okinawa

  • April 1, 1945 (~08:30 AM) Allied landings on Okinawa.

    The U.S. 10th Army (6 Marine and 4 Army divisions) lands on the Hagushi beaches on Okinawa’s west coast. A massive naval bombardment and air attack precede the assault. Japanese defenders (Ushijima’s 32nd Army) refuse to defend the coast and have withdrawn inland, so the beach assault meets almost no opposition. Within the first hour ~16,000 American troops are ashore; by nightfall ~50,000 total. At the same time, U.S. Marines stage a diversionary feint on the east coast (Minatoga Beach) which draws kamikaze attacks: Japanese suicide planes strike the decoy force, damaging USS Hinsdale and LST‑844 and causing ~50 Marine casualties. (Allied L‑Day casualties are relatively light due to the lack of beach defenses.)

  • April 4, 1945 U.S. forces split the island.

    Advancing inland, Army and Marine units have cut Okinawa in two by April 4. The northern half is largely secured; Japanese troops withdraw into the south (Shuri) strongholds. On this day kamikaze attacks intensify, but no major land counterattack yet occurs.

  • April 6-7, 1945 Operation Ten‑Go.

    Beginning April 6, the Japanese launch a mass kamikaze offensive, sending over 300 suicide planes against the invasion fleet. Simultaneously, Japan dispatches the battleship Yamato (escorted by destroyers) on a one‑way mission to attack the fleet. On April 7 U.S. carrier planes sink Yamato (with most of her crew) before she reaches Okinawa. The kamikazes inflict heavy naval losses: over the campaign 36 Allied ships are sunk and ~368 damaged (26 ships lost by mid‑May). About 40% of U.S. battle casualties occur at sea from these suicide attacks.

  • April 12, 1945 New Japanese suicide weapon appears.

    The Japanese debut the Baka piloted rocket (manned bomb). A Baka crashes into USS Abele off Okinawa, sinking the destroyer. (These attacks continue sporadically through June.)

  • April 18, 1945 American forces complete capture of northern Okinawa.

    U.S. troops and Marines have cleared the entire northern two‑thirds of the island by April 22. (Notably, war correspondent Ernie Pyle is killed on Ie Shima on April 18, highlighting the campaign’s toll.)

Shuri Line Battles

  • Late April-May, 1945 Battle for the Shuri Line.

    American units press south into heavily fortified Japanese defenses around Shuri (Okinawa’s medieval capital). Intense fighting is fought ridge by ridge (e.g. Kakazu, Sugar Loaf) along a series of prepared lines. Supply problems, monsoon rains, and murderous artillery make the advance slow and costly (American combat losses rise sharply during this period). The Japanese defense, anchored at Shuri Castle and its ridgelines, is largely under Lt. Gen. Ushijima’s 32nd Army, intent on inflicting maximum casualties as promised.

  • May 29, 1945 Capture of Shuri Castle.

    After weeks of bitter fighting, U.S. forces finally overrun Shuri Castle (the Japanese headquarters) on May 29. This breaks the main defensive line. Surviving Japanese troops retreat into the southernmost hills around Naha; many commit suicide rather than surrender.

  • June 4, 1945 Capture of Kanegusuku.

    U.S. Marines conduct a small amphibious landing to the west of Naha and secure the town’s airfield (Kanegusuku) on June 4. This move bypasses remaining Japanese positions and helps encircle the last defenders in southern Okinawa.

  • June 18, 1945 Death of Lt. Gen. Buckner Jr..

    Lt. Gen. Simon B. Buckner Jr., commander of the 10th Army, is killed by Japanese artillery fire while observing the front lines. Buckner’s death (during the final thrust south) is a major U.S. loss; he is the highest‑ranking American killed in World War II. Brig. Gen. Roy Geiger (USMC) assumes command briefly before Gen. J. Stilwell takes over on June 23.

  • June 21-22, 1945 End of organized resistance.

    On June 21 the last U.S. units link up on Okinawa’s south coast. The remaining Japanese garrison (now cut off) effectively ceases resistance on June 22. That same day Lt. Gen. Ushijima and his chief of staff Gen. Cho commit ritual suicide rather than surrender. The Battle of Okinawa is officially over after 82 days of fighting.

Outcome & Casualties

The assault secured Okinawa for the Allies but at enormous cost. U.S. forces suffered roughly 49,000 casualties (about 12,000 killed) during the campaign. The Japanese garrison was almost completely destroyed: of ~100,000 defenders, 90,000–100,000 were killed (most others were wounded or committed suicide). Okinawan civilian deaths were also catastrophic (on the order of 100,000), many forced by the Japanese to kill themselves. The ferocity of Okinawa convinced Allied leaders that a mainland invasion would be prohibitively bloody (a factor in the decision to use atomic bombs).
