What Is a Country?

You live in one, carry its passport, and cheer for it at the Olympics. Countries feel like the most natural thing in the world — they are permanent, obvious, and fixed on every map you've ever seen. But when pressed to define exactly what makes a country, most of us realize the answer is far more complicated than one can imagine.

GeoBoard Editorial
Published 5th April, 2026
7 min read
Chapter 1

The Simplest Question With No Simple Answer

There are 193 countries in the world. Or 195. It could also be 197. Actually, it depends on who you ask. The disagreement didn't appear overnight — in fact, it reflects one of the biggest tensions at the heart of international politics. The number changes based on something way more than a textbook definition.

Meet the United Nations

Almost the entire world looks to the United Nations for that answer. Inside the United Nations are 193 member states, most of them are fully recognized as countries, like the United States or France .

If you have a seat at the UN, you're a country. Simple enough — but to get into this country club, a state must join the UN in the first place. Let's say a country called Geoland wants to join. Its government sends a letter to the UN's Secretary-General basically saying they exist, they're a country, and they want to join the UN.

The Security Council, made up of 15 countries, then votes on whether to recommend Geoland as a member state. If Geoland gets 9 out of 15 votes in the Security Council saying "yay" instead of "nay", and no "nay"s from the 5 permanent members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States), then Geoland's proposal goes to the council of all 193 member states, called the General Assembly. There, Geoland needs a two-thirds majority vote to be admitted as a member state. Congratulations, Geoland is now a member of the United Nations, and a textbook example of a country!1

This process is relatively simple for international matters. However, the UN doesn't have a clear definition of what a country is, and it doesn't have a clear framework for solving disputes over recognition. So there are many entities that function as countries in practice, but aren't recognized by the UN as such. Examples include Taiwan , Kosovo , and even some super edge-cases like the State of Palestine — all of which are considered by some to be countries, but not all. So if they're not countries, what are they?2

Chapter 2

The Birth of the State

The answer to our question lies in the history of how states came to be.

It's 1648. Europe has just pulled itself through years and years of war, like the Eighty Years War and the Thirty Years War in the many German states that were under the rule of an Emperor.

The peace treaty that ended all these wars was the Peace of Westphalia, and its changes to Europe were huge. It established the principle of sovereignty, meaning that each state had exclusive authority over its own affairs and its relations with other states, as well as allowing their princes who ruled them to choose their own religion and political systems without external interference. The Holy Roman Empire now exercised less power over the states that made it up.3

Basically, the Peace of Westphalia made the Holy Roman Empire's states more independent, resembling modern nation-states, creating the pretext for all of modern statehood. This weakened the Holy Roman Empire, and it contributed to its eventual dissolution by Napoleon in 1806.

The HRE's states had clear identities, shared culture and defined government, which made Westphalia a lot easier to implement. For the next two and a half centuries, the Westphalian model spread — first across Europe, then through colonialism across the rest of the world. However by the 1920s, the states of the world were way more complex in their cultures and politics than they were back when Westphalia was signed. Tribes and ethnic groups were eyeing their colonial rulers, vying for independence and to rule themselves free of foreign control. So now, who qualified? Who got a seat at the table? The League of Nations, the international body created after WWI to prevent future wars, had no clear answer. Disputes over borders and recognition dragged on with no legal framework to resolve them.

Our countries in the Americas might give us the final answer.

Chapter 3

Montevideo

In 1933, Montevideo, Uruguay, 20 countries sat down and tried to answer this question once and for all. Their convention outlined four criteria for statehood that are still the starting point for every international law class today.4

To be or not to be a state...

Run an entity through the Montevideo Convention check.

Does your chosen country have a fixed land area and people living in it every day?

Does it have an operational government capable of making, enforcing, and protecting laws?

Does it possess the capacity to independently form international relations and treaties with other states?

⚖️

Verdict

The Montevideo criteria is the bare minimum for a country to have a chance at a seat at the UN. Recognition by others is the invisible fifth requirement.

This is the closest treaty outlining the criteria for a country. However, Montevideo is not bulletproof. Consider Taiwan . It has a government, borders, a currency, and a population. By Montevideo's measure, it qualifies. But it is recognized by only 12 UN member states. Why?

Turns out, other countries have a say as well...

Chapter 4

Recognition: The Politics of Existence

Recognition is — in a nutshell — a small nudge saying I acknowledge you exist. The recognizing nation may then form relations with it, or issue travel advisories, or engage in other forms of diplomatic interaction. Despite recognition seeming to be a simple act, it is profoundly political. Some people even say that recognition directly decides whether a country exists (called the constitutive view) as opposed to an optional criterion (the declaratory view). Whether a country recognizes another can sometimes reveal its alliances, history, and interests far more than any map can.56


Let's return to our example, Taiwan . It is recognized by only 12 UN member states — and yet it has a thriving democracy, a world-leading semiconductor industry, and a population of 23 million.

Turns out it did have more recognition in the past. Before 1949, Taiwan (then called the Republic of China) was recognized by most of the world as the legitimate government of all of China. But then, communists who opposed the Republic waged war aginst them, and won.


The Republic of China, however, continued to be recognised as the legitimate government of all of China — until 1971, when the UN transferred its seat to the newly powerful People's Republic of China (UN Resolution 2758). Most countries followed suit in recognizing the PRC, who also declared a "One China" policy, that stated that only one China existed, the PRC itself. Since then, countries have been increasingly attracted to China's growing economic influence, and so they have switched their recognition to the PRC. Taiwan's recognition has dwindled to just a few small countries in Central America and the Pacific.78

This is a complex and evolving issue, with implications that extend far beyond the simple question of statehood. Taiwan's story and goal for independence and recognition is controversial and is heavily debated. This entire story we just simplified heavily for you comes from a single act of recognition.

Chapter 5

The Edge Cases That Break Every Definition

This even applies to the UN itself. Some countries in the UN function more as a city, while some regions are distinct enough from their parent states to function as a country.

Vatican City has a population of around 800, making it the world's smallest state — yet it maintains diplomatic relations with over 180 countries. Despite its small size, it has an observer seat at the UN, embassies around the world and is the headquarters of the Catholic Church and over 1 billion faithful.

On the other end of the scale, Hong Kong , a city in southern China, is a 'Special Administrative Region' with a population of over 7.5 million. This once-British colony has its own culture and government distinct from Beijing, but is internationally considered part of China.

Then there are places that used to be countries: East Germany , Yugoslavia , and the Soviet Union , whose seats at the UN are now held by their successor states.

Conclusion

Why It Matters

So, did we answer our question? Not quite. But we've seen the complexity of the country, and this raises many other questions. Why do we have countries? What are the motives behind independence? What are the thoughts behind a country's decision to establish relations, or increase taxes, or expand on rights? Why do people allow themselves to be ruled under a capital city tens to hundreds of miles away?

How we define a country determines who gets a seat at the table when climate agreements are signed, who can appeal to international courts when their rights are violated, and whose citizens can travel freely.

Every line on the world map is shaped by years, decades and centuries of decisions made by citizens, generals and leaders. Analyzing these decisions is called political theory, and it's the first step to understanding the world.

The world map shows humanity's unity and divison, our greatest feats and our darkest scars. Behind every border is not just a jumble of political laws and treaties, but a history of how we got here, and where we're going.
